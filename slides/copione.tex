\section{Slides 1-3: Project Description}

\subsection{Slide 1 — Title Page}

\paragraph{Script.}
Good morning, we are Group R. Today we present our Computational Imaging
project: deblurring and denoising cervical cytology images acquired with
Liquid-Based Cytology (LBC). The project compares three fundamentally
different approaches --- variational, deep learning, and generative ---
on the same benchmark, so we can discuss not just the numbers but the
trade-offs each philosophy brings.

The full code is on GitHub at the link shown.

\paragraph{Concept.}
Why compare three methods? Each represents a different way to inject
\textit{prior knowledge} into an ill-posed problem. TV says ``images are
piecewise smooth.'' UNet says ``I learned what cells look like from data.''
DiffPIR says ``I know the distribution of clean images; I can generate
a plausible reconstruction.'' Putting them on the same test bed is the
real contribution: most papers compare in different conditions.

\subsection{Slide 2 — Inverse Problem}

\paragraph{Script.}
The degradation model is simple: we start with a clean image $x$, apply
Gaussian blur $\mathcal{H}$ ($\sigma=2$, kernel $9\times9$), add AWGN noise
$n$ at four levels ($\sigma_n=0.005$, $0.01$, $0.05$, $0.1$), and obtain
the observation $y$. Mathematically: $y = \mathcal{H}(x) + n$.

The inverse problem --- recover $x$ from $y$ --- is \textbf{ill-posed} in
the Hadamard sense. Three reasons:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Existence:} noise may push $y$ outside the range of
$\mathcal{H}$; no exact $x$ satisfies $y = \mathcal{H}(x)$.
\item \textbf{Uniqueness:} the blur is a low-pass filter --- it kills
high frequencies. Two different images that differ only in high
frequencies produce the same $y$.
\item \textbf{Stability:} the inverse of $\mathcal{H}$ is ill-conditioned
(bad condition number). Small changes in $y$ (noise) cause huge changes
in the estimate --- trying to invert directly amplifies noise catastrophically.
\end{enumerate}
We need \textbf{regularization}: extra information that makes the problem
well-posed. Each method regularizes differently.

\paragraph{Concept (why this matters).}
Ill-posedness is the fundamental reason we cannot just ``undo'' the blur.
Think of looking through frosted glass: you see shapes but not details.
You cannot remove the glass (invert $\mathcal{H}$) without amplifying every
speck of dust (noise). Regularization is like cleaning the glass without
scratching it. Each method has its own cleaning technique.

\subsection{Slide 3 — Selected Methods}

\paragraph{Script.}
We are two people, so we chose three out of four methods (excluding Weighted TV).

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Total Variation} --- variational, classical. Minimizes
$\|\mathcal{H}x - y\|^2 + \lambda \|\nabla x\|_1$. No training needed,
fully interpretable, but limited at high noise.
\item \textbf{UNet} --- deep learning. Learns $f_\theta(y) \approx x$
from degraded/clean pairs. Extremely fast inference (0.03 s/img), but
needs supervised training.
\item \textbf{DiffPIR} --- generative plug-and-play. Uses a DDPM as
a denoising prior, alternating between generative denoising and data
fidelity solved in the Fourier domain. Realistic textures but slow
($\sim$3 s/img) and hallucinates at low noise.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Concept (methodological families).}
These are not just three algorithms; they are three philosophical
approaches to solving inverse problems. Variational: hand-crafted prior.
Deep learning: learned prior, end-to-end. Generative: learned prior
on the distribution of clean images, then combined with the physical
degradation model. Comparing them reveals which situations favor
which philosophy.

\section{Slides 4-5: Dataset}

\subsection{Slide 4 — Mendeley LBC Cervical Cancer}

\paragraph{Script.}
The dataset has 962 cervical cytology images from Liquid-Based Cytology,
classified into four diagnostic categories: NILM (normal, 64\%),
HSIL, LSIL, and SCC (lesions). Original resolution is $2048\times1536$;
we resize to $256\times256$.

Why $256\times256$? It preserves cell morphology --- nuclei are about
$15\times15$ pixels, still visible --- while being small enough for
CPU training in reasonable time.

Class imbalance does not matter: we are restoring, not classifying.
Morphological features (nuclei, cytoplasm) appear in all classes, so
every image contributes equally to learning the restoration task.

\paragraph{Concept (fixing the why).}
The $2048\to256$ resize is a deliberate trade-off. At $256$, cell
nuclei (the diagnostically relevant structure) are still clearly
visible. At $128$ they'd be blurred into a single pixel. At $512$,
training would be 4x slower with marginal quality gain. We chose the
sweet spot for CPU feasibility.

\subsection{Slide 5 — Preprocessing and Split}

\paragraph{Script.}
The pipeline is identical for all methods (fair comparison):
\begin{enumerate}
\item Bilinear resize $2048\to256$.
\item ToTensor: pixel values $[0,255] \to [0,1]$ as floats.
\item Normalize to $[-1,1]$ via $(x-0.5)/0.5$. This range is required
by the DDPM (which assumes centered noise $\mathcal{N}(0,I)$) and works
well with MSE/L1 losses.
\item Stratified split 70\% train / 15\% val / 15\% test with seed 42.
\end{enumerate}
Every method sees the same 145 test images, degraded identically.

\paragraph{Concept (why $[-1,1]$).}
DDPMs are trained to predict Gaussian noise. If the image is in $[0,1]$,
the noise has nonzero mean relative to the image range; the model has
to learn both the noise \emph{and} the offset. Centering at zero
removes this coupling. Also, MSE on $[-1,1]$ has balanced gradients
across the dynamic range.

\section{Slide 6 — Degradation}

\paragraph{Script.}
Every test image undergoes the same degradation pipeline: first Gaussian
blur ($\sigma=2$, kernel $9\times9$), then AWGN at four levels:
$\sigma_n \in \{0.005, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1\}$. The result is clamped to
$[-1,1]$. Seed 42 for reproducibility.

These four levels represent qualitatively different regimes:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\sigma_n=0.005$: negligible noise ($\sim$0.5\% of dynamic range).
This is essentially a deblur-only problem.
\item $\sigma_n=0.01$: mild noise. TV and UNet still excel here.
\item $\sigma_n=0.05$: significant noise. The first regime shift;
TV begins to struggle (staircasing appears).
\item $\sigma_n=0.1$: heavy noise ($\sim$10\% of dynamic range).
Noise dominates; blur becomes secondary.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Concept (why four levels).}
We want to study the \textit{transition}. Classical methods degrade
with noise; DiffPIR, counterintuitively, improves. Four levels let us
plot performance curves and see where the methods cross. This is more
informative than a single noise level.

\section{Slides 7-9: Total Variation}

\subsection{Slide 7 — TV Theory}

\paragraph{Script.}
TV minimizes a two-term functional:
\[
\hat{x} = \arg\min_x \|\mathcal{H}x - y\|_2^2 \;+\; \lambda \cdot \|\nabla x\|_1
\]
The first term is data fidelity: how well does the estimate, after blur,
match the observation? The second is regularization: the total variation
of the image, the L1 norm of the gradient.

The key design choice is \textbf{L1 instead of L2}. If we used L2
(Tikhonov regularization), the solution would be smooth --- edges get
smeared. L1 promotes \textbf{sparsity in the gradient}: most gradient
values are zero (uniform regions), a few are large (edges). This keeps
sharp edges.

\paragraph{Concept (L1 vs L2, explained intuitively).}
Think of a step edge: 1 pixel with value 1, surrounded by zeros.
\begin{itemize}
\item L1: $|1| = 1$.
\item L2: $1^2 = 1$ (same).
\end{itemize}
Now spread that edge over 10 pixels (a ramp): each pixel has value 0.1.
\begin{itemize}
\item L1: $10 \times 0.1 = 1$ (same as the sharp edge).
\item L2: $10 \times 0.01 = 0.1$ (10x smaller!)
\end{itemize}
L2 \textit{prefers} the ramp because it penalizes less. But we want
the sharp edge! L1 treats both equally, so it does not penalize
sharpness. This is why L1 preserves edges and L2 smooths them.

The functional is convex (quadratic + L1 norm), which guarantees a
unique global optimum --- a property deep learning methods do not have.

\subsection{Slide 8 — TV Algorithm}

\paragraph{Script.}
We initialize $x = y$ and optimize with Adam (lr $10^{-3}$) for 150
iterations. At each step: compute the blurred estimate $\mathcal{H}x$,
compute the loss (fidelity + $\lambda \cdot TV(x)$), backpropagate,
update with Adam, clamp to $[-1,1]$.

Why Adam? TV gradients have variable magnitude: large at edges, small
in uniform regions. Adam adapts the learning rate per pixel --- it
accelerates in flat areas (where gradients are small) and brakes at
edges (where gradients are large). Standard SGD would need a single
learning rate for all pixels, which is suboptimal.

\paragraph{Concept (adaptivity).}
Think of driving in a city: Adam accelerates on the highway (uniform
regions, small gradient) and brakes in traffic (edges, large gradient).
SGD drives at a constant speed everywhere. This is why Adam converges
faster for TV optimization.

After validation, $\lambda=0.005$ gives the best balance. Too high:
staircasing dominates. Too low: residual noise remains.

\subsection{Slide 9 — TV Results}

\paragraph{Script.}
PSNR $>$ 32 dB and SSIM $>$ 0.9 at $\sigma_n \leq 0.01$ --- excellent,
without any training. At $\sigma_n=0.05$, PSNR drops to 30.42 dB,
SSIM to 0.837: staircasing becomes visible. At $\sigma_n=0.1$, PSNR
is 26.54 dB, SSIM collapses to 0.586 --- staircasing distorts the
local structure.

\paragraph{Concept (why SSIM collapses more than PSNR).}
PSNR measures pixel-level error; SSIM measures structural similarity
(luminance, contrast, structure) in local windows. Staircasing creates
``steps'' where there should be smooth gradients. The pixel values are
still roughly correct (PSNR holds up), but the local structure is
destroyed (SSIM plummets). This is why you need both metrics: PSNR
lies, SSIM reveals.

\section{Slides 10-12: UNet}

\subsection{Slide 10 — UNet Architecture}

\paragraph{Script.}
The UNet is an encoder-decoder with skip connections. The encoder
compresses the image: 16 $\to$ 32 $\to$ 64 $\to$ 128 $\to$ 256
channels, halving spatial resolution at each level with max-pooling.
The bottleneck is 256 channels at $16\times16$. The decoder mirrors
the encoder, halving channels and upsampling.

Skip connections concatenate the encoder feature map with the decoder
feature map at the same resolution level. This is the key innovation:
without them, the bottleneck would lose spatial detail --- the decoder
would know \textit{what} is in the image but not \textit{where}.
Skip connections preserve localization.

GroupNorm: divides 16 channels into 4 groups of 4, normalizes each
group to zero mean and unit variance. Better than BatchNorm when
batch size is small (we use 16), because the estimate is local to
the sample, not across the batch.

\paragraph{Concept (why skip connections matter).}
A painter keeps the reference photo next to the canvas: looks at the
subject (encoder), paints (decoder), but constantly checks the original
details (skip connections). Without the photo, the painter would
remember the subject's essence but forget exactly where the eyes go.

\subsection{Slide 11 — UNet Training}

\paragraph{Script.}
Loss: L1 (MAE) instead of MSE. Why? MSE penalizes large errors
quadratically, pushing the model toward conservative (smoothed)
solutions. L1 penalizes linearly, preserving fine details like
cell nuclei. For medical images, detail preservation matters more
than overall pixel accuracy.

Multi-noise augmentation: at each batch, $\sigma_n$ is uniformly
sampled from $\{0.005, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1\}$. This forces the model
to learn a denoising strategy that works across the entire noise
spectrum. Without it, the model would overfit to a single noise
level.

Hyperparameters: Adam lr $10^{-4}$, batch size 16, 50 epochs.
Training on CPU takes a few hours.

\paragraph{Concept (why L1 over MSE).}
MSE is unforgiving: a large error on a single pixel (like a sharp
nucleus boundary) contributes massively to the loss. The model learns
to avoid any large error by smoothing everything. L1 says ``a big
mistake is bad, but not catastrophic'' --- it accepts sharp
predictions even if they occasionally miss.

\subsection{Slide 12 — UNet Results}

\paragraph{Script.}
Two remarkable results. First: PSNR varies by only $\sim$1 dB across
all noise levels (28.46-29.79 dB). The model learned a truly
noise-invariant strategy, thanks to the multi-noise augmentation.
Second: inference takes 0.03 s/img --- 200x faster than TV, 100x
faster than DiffPIR. Once trained, UNet is a single forward pass.

At $\sigma_n=0.1$, UNet surpasses TV by nearly 2 dB (28.46 vs 26.54)
and 0.21 SSIM (0.795 vs 0.586). At low noise, TV is slightly ahead:
the learned prior generalizes, but it pays a small accuracy cost on
``easy'' data.

\paragraph{Concept (the inflection point).}
UNet is the best trade-off overall. Not the absolute best at any
single metric, but the most robust across the entire noise spectrum.
It is the pragmatic choice for a production system: fast, consistent,
reliable.

\section{Slides 13-16: DiffPIR}

\subsection{Slide 13 — DiffPIR Overview}

\paragraph{Script.}
DiffPIR (CVPR 2023) integrates a diffusion model into a Plug-and-Play
framework. It alternates two steps: denoising (via DDPM) and
data-fidelity (solved analytically in the Fourier domain).

The key deviation from the original DiffPIR: instead of using a
pretrained ImageNet model (500M parameters, 2 GB), we trained a
\textbf{LightUNet} from scratch on 673 cervical LBC images. It has
1.26M parameters ($\sim$5 MB) --- small enough for CPU inference.

\paragraph{Concept (why custom LightUNet).}
An ImageNet model knows about dogs, cars, and landscapes --- not about
cell nuclei. Training on cervical cytology makes the prior specific
to the domain. And 500M parameters would be infeasible on CPU;
1.26M is not. This is a deliberate trade-off: quality vs.
practicality.

\subsection{Slide 14 — DDPM and LightUNet}

\paragraph{Script.}
The DDPM (Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model, Ho et al.\
NeurIPS 2020) works in two phases:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Forward:} gradually add Gaussian noise over 1000
timesteps, with a linear scheduler $\beta_1=10^{-4}$, $\beta_T=0.02$.
After 1000 steps, the image is pure noise.
\item \textbf{Reverse:} learn to remove noise step by step. The model
predicts the noise $\varepsilon$ added at each step (not the clean
image $x_0$).
\end{itemize}

The LightUNet architecture: 32 base channels, encoder 32$\to$64$\to$128,
sinusoidal time embedding + MLP (like in Transformers) to encode which
timestep $t$ we are at. GroupNorm + SiLU activations. 30 epochs of
training on 673 clean images (no degradation --- just learning the
distribution of clean LBC images).

\paragraph{Concept (why predict noise, not the image).}
This is subtle but fundamental. Predicting $x_0$ directly is hard:
$x_0$ lives in a complex, high-dimensional manifold (real images).
Predicting $\varepsilon$ is easy: $\varepsilon$ has a known, simple
distribution (standard Gaussian). The model learns to ``remove the
dirt'' rather than ``draw from scratch.'' It is the difference between
teaching someone to restore an old photograph (remove scratches, fix
fading) versus teaching them to recreate the photograph from memory.

The time embedding tells the model how dirty the image is: ``this is
slightly dirty, just polish it'' ($t$ small) vs.\ ``this is completely
degraded, do a major restoration'' ($t$ large).

DDIM sampling (Song et al., ICLR 2021) reduces the 1000 steps to 15,
accelerating inference by $\sim$60x with minimal quality loss.

\subsection{Slide 15 — DiffPIR Algorithm}

\paragraph{Script.}
At each iteration (starting from $t_{\text{start}}=50$, down to 0, in
15 DDIM steps):
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Denoising:} given current noisy image $x_t$, the DDPM
predicts the noise $\varepsilon$ and estimates the clean image
$\hat{x}_0^{(t)}$.
\item \textbf{Data fidelity (Wiener in FFT):} reconciles the prior
estimate $\hat{x}_0$ with the observation $y$:
\[
\tilde{x}_0 = \mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\left(
\frac{\mathcal{F}(y)\,\overline{\mathcal{F}(k)} + \rho_t\,\mathcal{F}(\hat{x}_0)}
{|\mathcal{F}(k)|^2 + \rho_t}
\right)
\]
\item \textbf{DDIM step:} from $\tilde{x}_0$, sample $x_{t-1}$ for
the next iteration.
\end{enumerate}

The weight $\rho_t$ is dynamic:
\[
\rho_t = \lambda \cdot \frac{\sigma^2 \cdot \bar{\alpha}_t}{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}
\]
When $t$ is large (heavy noise), $\rho_t$ is large --- we trust the
prior. When $t$ is small (near clean), $\rho_t$ is small --- we trust
the data.

\paragraph{Concept (adaptive balancing).}
$\rho_t$ is like a referee: at the start of the game (high noise,
chaos), the referee lets the players play (prior dominates). At the
end (low noise), the referee calls more fouls (data takes over). The
gradual transition prevents sudden jumps.

The FFT solution is crucial: a naive Wiener filter would require
solving an $O(N^3)$ linear system. The FFT makes it $O(N \log N)$.
This is possible because the blur is a convolution --- it becomes
a pointwise multiplication in the Fourier domain.

\subsection{Slide 16 — DiffPIR Results}

\paragraph{Script.}
DiffPIR has an inverted behavior: PSNR \textbf{increases} with noise.
From 15.78 dB at $\sigma_n=0.005$ to 25.46 dB at $\sigma_n=0.1$.
SSIM follows the same trend: 0.329 $\to$ 0.766.

This is the exact opposite of TV and UNet. Why?

\paragraph{Concept (bias-variance trade-off --- the key insight).}
DiffPIR has a strong generative prior (high bias). The prior says ``the
image must look like the LBC images I was trained on.'' At low noise,
the data already carries good information --- but the prior overrides
it, \textbf{hallucinating} details that are realistic but not present
in the ground truth. This is why PSNR is terrible at $\sigma_n=0.005$:
the model is inventing textures that look plausible but are wrong.

At high noise, the data is too corrupted to be useful alone. The prior
is no longer a liability --- it becomes an asset: it ``fills in'' the
missing information with realistic structure, reducing variance.
This is the classic bias-variance trade-off: high bias hurts when data
is abundant, but helps when data is scarce.

A concrete analogy: two photographers at a concert. The first (high
bias) only knows how to shoot rock concerts. If the concert is rock,
photos are perfect. If it is jazz, they force everything to look like
rock --- terrible. The second (low bias) can photograph any genre but
is unstable in low light (high noise). DiffPIR is the first
photographer: perfect if the domain matches, hallucinates if it does
not. At low noise, the genre is known well enough that the prior
``jazzifies'' the image unnecessarily.

\section{Slide 17 — Implementation}

\paragraph{Script.}
The repository is modular: each method is an independent module in
\texttt{src/methods/}. The shared pipeline handles degradation,
metrics, and plotting. 34 unit tests verify core functionality.
Seed 42 guarantees exact reproducibility.

Execution steps:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \texttt{preprocess.py} --- resize and split
\item \texttt{run\_tv.py} --- variational deblurring
\item \texttt{run\_unet.py} --- train and evaluate UNet
\item \texttt{run\_diffpir.py} --- generative restoration
\item \texttt{plot\_results.py} --- metrics and visualizations
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Concept (why modularity matters).}
A shared pipeline means the comparison is truly fair: the same
degradation, same test set, same metrics. Many papers compare methods
trained on different data with different preprocessing. Our setup
eliminates this confounding factor.

(luminance, contrast, texture). TV's staircasing shifts pixels
slightly (PSNR drops moderately) but destroys the local adjacency
relationships (SSIM crashes). DiffPIR's hallucinations create
``plausible'' local structure (SSIM is decent) but the pixels
are simply wrong (bad PSNR).

\subsection{Slide 23 — $\sigma_n=0.1$}

\paragraph{Script.}

The \textit{pattern} of error reveals the failure mode. TV's error
is structured (systematic bias from the wrong prior). UNet's error is
uniform (noise-like, the model is confident). DiffPIR's error is
localized and structured (the model is confidently wrong about
specific features). This is the visual signature of hallucination.

\section{Slides 24-25: Discussion}

\subsection{Slide 24 — Family Comparison}

\paragraph{Script.}
Each family has distinct trade-offs:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{TV:} no training, interpretable, unique global optimum
(convex). Excellent at low noise. Achilles' heel: staircasing at high
noise. Inference: $\sim$7 s.
\item \textbf{UNet:} very fast inference (0.03 s/img), robust across
the noise spectrum. Requires supervised training, limited generalization
outside the training distribution.
\item \textbf{DiffPIR:} realistic textures, improves with noise, does
not require degraded/clean pairs (only clean images for the DDPM).
Slow ($\sim$3 s/img), hallucinates at low noise.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Concept (no universal winner).}
No method dominates all regimes. This is the honest answer: the best
choice depends on the noise level, computational budget, and whether
you prioritize pixel accuracy or perceptual quality. Deep learning is
not always the answer; TV at low noise beats all other methods with
zero training.

\subsection{Slide 25 — Operating Regimes}

\paragraph{Script.}
Summary recommendations:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\sigma_n < 0.05$ (low noise): TV. Excellent quality, no training.
\item $\sigma_n = 0.05$ (medium noise): UNet. Robust, fast.
\item $\sigma_n = 0.1$ (high noise): UNet (best metrics) or DiffPIR
(if texture quality is valued over pixel accuracy).
\end{itemize}
Inference speed: UNet (0.03 s) $\gg$ DiffPIR (3 s) $>$ TV (7 s).

% SECTION 11: CONCLUSIONS (Slide 26)
% =============================================================================
\section{Slide 26 — Conclusions}

\paragraph{Script.}
Three main results:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{UNet is the best practical trade-off:} robust across the
noise spectrum, extremely fast, good metrics. Not the absolute best
in every cell, but the most balanced.
\item \textbf{The fair comparison is the real contribution:} shared
pipeline, same metrics, same test set. Most papers do not control for
these factors.
\item \textbf{No method is universally superior:} the choice is
context-dependent. TV without training beats deep learning at low noise.
DiffPIR offers a generative alternative for high-noise scenarios.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Concept (the takeaway).}
The project is not about finding the ``best'' method. It is about
understanding \textit{when} each philosophy works. This is the
essence of computational imaging: the problem is ill-posed, so the
prior --- whether hand-crafted, learned, or generative --- determines
everything.
